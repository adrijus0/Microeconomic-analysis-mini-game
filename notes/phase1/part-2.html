Allais paradox: a rare drop in OSRS (vote!)

Old School RuneScape: you got a rare drop. How do you cash it in?

Question 1. Which do you take?

  • $A$: sell on the Grand Exchange: 2 400k gp for sure.
  • $B$: walk it through the Wilderness to a buyer: 2 500k gp w.p. 0.33, 2 400k w.p. 0.66, a player killer takes it w.p. 0.01.

Question 2. Two deep-Wilderness trips. Which do you take?

  • $C$: 2 500k gp w.p. 0.33, nothing w.p. 0.67.
  • $D$: 2 400k gp w.p. 0.34, nothing w.p. 0.66.
Typical answers (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979)
82% choose $A$ and 83% choose $C$.

Why $A\succ B$ and $C\succ D$ is inconsistent

Amounts in thousands of gp; normalize $u(0)=0$. Expected utility gives:

$$ \begin{align*} A\succ B &\iff u(2400) > 0.33\,u(2500)+0.66\,u(2400)\\ &\iff 0.34\,u(2400) > 0.33\,u(2500),\\[4pt] C\succ D &\iff 0.33\,u(2500) > 0.34\,u(2400). \end{align*} $$ $$ 0.34\,u(2400)>0.33\,u(2500)>0.34\,u(2400)\qquad \bot $$
Common consequence
$C,D$ are $A,B$ with the common part “2 400 w.p. 0.66” replaced by “nothing w.p. 0.66”. Independence says this cannot matter; people react because $A$ is certain.

The probability triangle (Marschak–Machina)

  • Points = lotteries over $\{0,2400,2500\}$.
  • EU indifference curves: parallel lines (dashed).
  • $AB \parallel DC$, so EU ranks both pairs alike: $A\succ B \Leftrightarrow D\succ C$.
  • $A$ and $C$ together need curves that fan out.

Paradoxical pattern of choice

Consider four lotteries $L_1,L_2,L_3,L_4 \in \mathcal{L}$ over outcomes $x_1,x_2,x_3 \in \mathcal{X}$.

Each lottery is represented by a probability distribution:

$$ L_j=\{(x_i,p_{ij})\}_{i=1}^3, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^3p_{ij}=1. $$

Under expected utility, these preferences require

$$ \sum_{i=1}^3p_{i1}u(x_i)> \sum_{i=1}^3p_{i2}u(x_i), $$ $$ \sum_{i=1}^3p_{i4}u(x_i)> \sum_{i=1}^3p_{i3}u(x_i). $$
Question
Can a single utility function satisfy both inequalities while respecting independence?

Independence and inconsistent preferences

Without loss of generality, suppose the observed preferences satisfy

$$ L_1\succ L_2,\qquad L_4\succ L_3. $$

For some $\alpha\in(0,1)$, let

$$ L_3=\alpha L_1+(1-\alpha)N,\qquad L_4=\alpha L_2+(1-\alpha)N. $$

By the independence axiom, $L_1\succ L_2$ implies $L_3\succ L_4$. This contradicts the observed preference $L_4\succ L_3$.

Conclusion
The observed reversal violates independence: adding the same lottery component in the same proportions cannot reverse the preference ranking.

Generalized Allais paradox

Let $x_H>x_M>x_0$, $p,q\in(0,1)$, and define

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{P}_A&=\delta_{x_M},& \mathcal{P}_B&=p\delta_{x_H}+(1-p)\delta_{x_0},\\ \mathcal{P}_C&=q\delta_{x_M}+(1-q)\delta_{x_0},& \mathcal{P}_D&=pq\delta_{x_H}+(1-pq)\delta_{x_0}. \end{aligned} $$

Suppose

$$ \mathcal{P}_A\succ\mathcal{P}_B, \qquad \mathcal{P}_D\succ\mathcal{P}_C. $$

Expected utility, with $u(x_0)=0$, implies

$$ \begin{aligned} u(x_M)&>p\,u(x_H),\\ pq\,u(x_H)&>q\,u(x_M) \;\Longrightarrow\; p\,u(x_H)>u(x_M). \end{aligned} $$ $$ \boxed{u(x_M)>p\,u(x_H)>u(x_M)} \qquad\bot $$
$\text{Violation of independence} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{no expected-utility representation.}$
