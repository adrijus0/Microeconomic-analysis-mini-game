Choices under uncertainty

A lottery specifies possible outcomes and their probabilities:

$$L=\{(x_1,p_1),\ldots,(x_n,p_n)\}, \quad p_i\geq 0,\quad \sum_{i=1}^n p_i=1,\quad x_i\in\mathcal{X}.$$
  • Outcomes may be monetary prizes; probabilities describe their chances.
  • A decision-maker has preferences over lotteries, written $L\succeq M$ if $L$ is weakly preferred to $M$.
  • The question: can these preferences be represented by a consistent rule?
Expected value is not enough
Two lotteries can have the same expected monetary payoff but be ranked differently because the decision-maker responds to risk.

Example: a lottery in Minecraft

Mining a diamond ore with a Fortune III pickaxe

The game draws the number of diamonds you get:

$$L_{\text{mine}}=\Big\{(1,\tfrac25),\ (2,\tfrac15),\ (3,\tfrac15),\ (4,\tfrac15)\Big\}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[L_{\text{mine}}]=\tfrac{2+2+3+4}{5}=2.2 .$$

A villager offers instead: 2 diamonds for sure for the unmined ore.

  • Risk-neutral player: $2.2>2$ $\Rightarrow$ mine it.
  • A careful hardcore player who needs exactly 2 diamonds may take the sure 2.
  • Both are reasonable: we need a rule that ranks lotteries, not just their averages.

Expected utility

Assign each outcome $x$ a utility $u(x)$, where $u:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$. The expected utility of a lottery is

$$EU(L)=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i u(x_i).$$

The model predicts that

$$L\succeq M \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad EU(L)\geq EU(M).$$

This representation is justified by the von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility theorem. Preferences must be:

  • complete and transitive,
  • continuous, and
  • satisfy independence.
Independence: the key axiom

If $L\succeq M$, then for any lottery $N$ and $0<\alpha<1$,

$$\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N \succeq \alpha M+(1-\alpha)N.$$

Adding the same lottery component to both options should not reverse their ranking.

Independence axiom

Suppose $L\succeq M$. Independence requires

$$\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N \succeq \alpha M+(1-\alpha)N, \qquad \alpha>0.$$

Under expected utility, the difference between the two mixtures is

$$\begin{aligned} &EU\bigl(\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N\bigr)\\ &\quad-EU\bigl(\alpha M+(1-\alpha)N\bigr)\\ &=\alpha EU(L)+(1-\alpha)EU(N)\\ &\quad-\alpha EU(M)-(1-\alpha)EU(N)\\ &=\alpha\bigl[EU(L)-EU(M)\bigr]. \end{aligned}$$

Since $\alpha>0$, the sign of the difference is unchanged.

Prediction
A common lottery component cannot reverse the ranking between two lotteries.

Independence in plain words: a Skyrim dungeon

Two chests behind a door
W.p. $1-\alpha$ a draugr ambush ends the run (lottery $N$). W.p. $\alpha$ you reach the treasure room and pick chest $L$ (500 gold for sure) or chest $M$ (0 or 1200 gold, 50–50).
  • The ambush does not depend on the chest you pick.
  • If you prefer $L$ in the room, you should prefer “door, then $L$” to “door, then $M$”:
    $\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N\succeq\alpha M+(1-\alpha)N$.
The paradoxes
In experiments people systematically break this rule.

St. Petersburg paradox: the villager's coin

A villager offers a game
A coin is flipped until the first tails. If tails first appears on flip $k$, you win $2^k$ emeralds. How many emeralds would you pay to play?
$$\Pr(k)=2^{-k},\qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{prize}]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\cdot 2^{k}=1+1+1+\dots=\infty .$$
  • A risk-neutral player should pay any price.
  • In practice almost nobody pays more than $\approx$ 5–20 emeralds.
Paradox
Expected value gives an absurd answer (Bernoulli, 1738).

Bernoulli’s resolution: concave utility

With wealth $w$ and $u(x)=\ln x$, the maximum price $p$ solves

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\ln\!\big(w+2^{k}-p\big)=\ln w . $$
Wealth $w$Price $p$
104.97
1007.79
1 00010.95
1 000 00020.87

(numerical solution, see Appendix)

  • Each extra emerald is worth less: $u''<0$.
  • If the villager owns only $2^{20}\approx10^6$ emeralds, the expected value is just $21$.
Takeaway
This paradox created EU. But prizes $e^{2^k}$ break log utility again $\Rightarrow$ $u$ must be bounded.

Allais paradox: a rare drop in OSRS (vote!)

Old School RuneScape: you got a rare drop. How do you cash it in?

Question 1. Which do you take?

  • $A$: sell on the Grand Exchange: 2 400k gp for sure.
  • $B$: walk it through the Wilderness to a buyer: 2 500k gp w.p. 0.33, 2 400k w.p. 0.66, a player killer takes it w.p. 0.01.

Question 2. Two deep-Wilderness trips. Which do you take?

  • $C$: 2 500k gp w.p. 0.33, nothing w.p. 0.67.
  • $D$: 2 400k gp w.p. 0.34, nothing w.p. 0.66.
Typical answers (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979)
82% choose $A$ and 83% choose $C$.

Why $A\succ B$ and $C\succ D$ is inconsistent

Amounts in thousands of gp; normalize $u(0)=0$. Expected utility gives:

$$ \begin{align*} A\succ B &\iff u(2400) > 0.33\,u(2500)+0.66\,u(2400)\\ &\iff 0.34\,u(2400) > 0.33\,u(2500),\\[4pt] C\succ D &\iff 0.33\,u(2500) > 0.34\,u(2400). \end{align*} $$ $$ 0.34\,u(2400)>0.33\,u(2500)>0.34\,u(2400)\qquad \bot $$
Common consequence
$C,D$ are $A,B$ with the common part “2 400 w.p. 0.66” replaced by “nothing w.p. 0.66”. Independence says this cannot matter; people react because $A$ is certain.

The probability triangle (Marschak–Machina)

  • Points = lotteries over $\{0,2400,2500\}$.
  • EU indifference curves: parallel lines (dashed).
  • $AB \parallel DC$, so EU ranks both pairs alike: $A\succ B \Leftrightarrow D\succ C$.
  • $A$ and $C$ together need curves that fan out.

Paradoxical pattern of choice

Consider four lotteries $L_1,L_2,L_3,L_4 \in \mathcal{L}$ over outcomes $x_1,x_2,x_3 \in \mathcal{X}$.

Each lottery is represented by a probability distribution:

$$ L_j=\{(x_i,p_{ij})\}_{i=1}^3, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^3p_{ij}=1. $$

Under expected utility, these preferences require

$$ \sum_{i=1}^3p_{i1}u(x_i)> \sum_{i=1}^3p_{i2}u(x_i), $$ $$ \sum_{i=1}^3p_{i4}u(x_i)> \sum_{i=1}^3p_{i3}u(x_i). $$
Question
Can a single utility function satisfy both inequalities while respecting independence?

Independence and inconsistent preferences

Without loss of generality, suppose the observed preferences satisfy

$$ L_1\succ L_2,\qquad L_4\succ L_3. $$

For some $\alpha\in(0,1)$, let

$$ L_3=\alpha L_1+(1-\alpha)N,\qquad L_4=\alpha L_2+(1-\alpha)N. $$

By the independence axiom, $L_1\succ L_2$ implies $L_3\succ L_4$. This contradicts the observed preference $L_4\succ L_3$.

Conclusion
The observed reversal violates independence: adding the same lottery component in the same proportions cannot reverse the preference ranking.

Generalized Allais paradox

Let $x_H>x_M>x_0$, $p,q\in(0,1)$, and define

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{P}_A&=\delta_{x_M},& \mathcal{P}_B&=p\delta_{x_H}+(1-p)\delta_{x_0},\\ \mathcal{P}_C&=q\delta_{x_M}+(1-q)\delta_{x_0},& \mathcal{P}_D&=pq\delta_{x_H}+(1-pq)\delta_{x_0}. \end{aligned} $$

Suppose

$$ \mathcal{P}_A\succ\mathcal{P}_B, \qquad \mathcal{P}_D\succ\mathcal{P}_C. $$

Expected utility, with $u(x_0)=0$, implies

$$ \begin{aligned} u(x_M)&>p\,u(x_H),\\ pq\,u(x_H)&>q\,u(x_M) \;\Longrightarrow\; p\,u(x_H)>u(x_M). \end{aligned} $$ $$ \boxed{u(x_M)>p\,u(x_H)>u(x_M)} \qquad\bot $$
$\text{Violation of independence} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{no expected-utility representation.}$

Common ratio effect: a Minecraft expedition

Two plans for getting diamonds

$P_A$: villager trade, 30 diamonds for sure.
$P_B$: deep cave, 40 diamonds w.p. 0.8 (a creeper w.p. 0.2).

Same plans, but you first need to find a stronghold (w.p. $q=0.25$):
$P_C$: 30 w.p. 0.25  $P_D$: 40 w.p. $0.25\times0.8=0.20$.

  • $x_H=40$, $x_M=30$, $x_0=0$, $p=0.8$, $q=0.25$.
  • Typical: $P_A\succ P_B$ (“sure thing!”) but $P_D\succ P_C$ (“20% vs 25%, go for 40”).
Scaling all winning chances by $q$ should not change the choice — but it does.

Ellsberg paradox: the Khajiit’s sack of gems

A merchant’s sack holds 90 gems

30 rubies; 60 emeralds or sapphires in an unknown mix. Draw one blindly; win 100 gold if your bet is right.

BetRuby (30)Emerald (?)Sapphire (?)
I10000
II01000
III1000100
IV0100100
Typical choice
I $\succ$ II (“I know it is 1/3”) and IV $\succ$ III (“I know it is 2/3”).

Why no beliefs can explain it

Let $\pi_R=\tfrac13$, $\pi_E$, $\pi_S$ be the player’s subjective probabilities, $\pi_E+\pi_S=\tfrac23$.

$$\begin{align*} \text{I}\succ\text{II} &\iff \pi_R\,u(100) > \pi_E\,u(100) \iff \pi_R>\pi_E,\\ \text{IV}\succ\text{III} &\iff (\pi_E+\pi_S)\,u(100) > (\pi_R+\pi_S)\,u(100) \iff \pi_E>\pi_R . \end{align*}$$
  • Contradiction for every belief and every $u$.
  • “Sapphire” is a common consequence $\Rightarrow$ Savage’s sure-thing principle fails.
Risk vs. ambiguity
Allais: known odds, non-linear attitude to risk.
Ellsberg: unknown odds, people dislike ambiguity.

Framing: Whiterun under dragon attack

A dragon will kill 600 citizens unless the Jarl acts

Frame 1 (lives saved):

  • Plan A: 200 people will be saved.[72%]
  • Plan B: 1/3 chance 600 saved, 2/3 chance nobody saved.[28%]

Frame 2 (deaths):

  • Plan C: 400 people will die.[22%]
  • Plan D: 1/3 chance nobody dies, 2/3 chance 600 die.[78%]
Paradox (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981; Lecture 5)
A $\equiv$ C and B $\equiv$ D. Only the words changed, yet the majority switches: risk-averse for gains, risk-seeking for losses.

Loss aversion and small bets: hardcore mode

A 50–50 coin bet in a hardcore world

Lose 1 000 emeralds or win 1 050 emeralds. Do you accept?

  • Most say no, although the expected value is $+25$.
  • Rabin (2000): rejecting it at every wealth level forces EU to reject a 50–50 bet: lose 20 000 or win any amount. Absurd.
Explanation: losses hurt more than gains please
With $\lambda\approx2.25$: $0.5\cdot1050-0.5\cdot2.25\cdot1000=-600<0$ $\Rightarrow$ reject.
Losing your inventory hurts more than finding the same loot.

Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky)

$$V(L)=\sum_i w(p_i)\,v(x_i),\quad v(x)=\begin{cases}x^{\alpha}, & x\ge0\\ -\lambda(-x)^{\beta}, & x<0\end{cases},\quad w(p)=\frac{p^{\gamma}}{\big(p^{\gamma}+(1-p)^{\gamma}\big)^{1/\gamma}}$$

$x$ = gain/loss vs. a reference point; $\alpha=\beta=0.88$, $\lambda=2.25$, $\gamma=0.61$.

concave for gains, convex and steeper for losses

small $p$ overweighted, large $p$ underweighted

Ambiguity aversion fixes the gem sack

Maxmin expected utility (Gilboa & Schmeidler, 1989): the player considers a set of possible beliefs and judges each bet by the worst one.

  • Possible beliefs: $\pi_R=\tfrac13$, $\pi_E\in[0,\tfrac23]$, $\pi_S=\tfrac23-\pi_E$.
  • Worst-case winning chance of each bet:
Bet I II III IV
Worst-case chance $\tfrac13$ $0$ $\tfrac13$ $\tfrac23$
Result
I $\succ$ II and IV $\succ$ III — exactly the observed choices.

Conclusion

  • EU rests on the independence axiom: parallel, linear indifference curves.
  • St. Petersburg: we need utility, not expected value.
  • Allais / common ratio: certainty and probabilities are weighted non-linearly.
  • Ellsberg: people dislike unknown odds.
  • Framing / loss aversion: choices depend on a reference point.
  • Prospect theory, RDU and maxmin EU explain the patterns; EU stays the normative benchmark.
In game terms
Players love sure loot, fear losing their inventory, and avoid chests with unknown odds.

Appendix: St. Petersburg computation

○

Payoff $2^k$ w.p. $2^{-k}$, $k=1,2,\dots$; utility $u(x)=\ln x$; wealth $w$.

○

Willingness to pay $p$ is the root of

$$f(p)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}2^{-k}\ln\!\big(w+2^k-p\big)-\ln w=0,$$

truncated at $K=200$ (the tail is negligible), solved with Brent's method.

○

Finite bankroll $B=2^{n}$: payoff $\min(2^k,B)$, so

$$\mathbb{E}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}1+\sum_{k>n}2^{-k}2^{n}=n+1 .$$

With $B=2^{20}$: $\mathbb{E}=21$; with $B=2^{30}\approx10^9$: $\mathbb{E}=31$.
